How does a room change a sound?

Clap your hands in a bathroom and then in a bedroom, and the two claps sound different even though your hands did the same thing. We want to understand that difference in signal-processing terms, measure it in two real rooms, and reproduce it in simulation.

Yiheng Li · Yongjia Xia · Yingqian Sun

A room is a filter

Between a sound source and a listener, a room behaves approximately like a linear time-invariant system. Everything it does to a sound is captured by one signal, its impulse response \(h[n]\). What you hear is the original sound convolved with it:

\[ y[n] = x[n] * h[n] \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Y(e^{j\omega}) = X(e^{j\omega})\,H(e^{j\omega}) \]

An impulse response begins with the direct sound, continues with early reflections off walls, floor, ceiling and furniture, and ends in a reverberant tail that dies away roughly exponentially. This one equation lets us go both ways. Given \(h[n]\), we can make any dry recording sound as if it were played in that room. Given what we played, \(x[n]\), and what we recorded, \(y[n]\), we can work backwards to the room's \(h[n]\).

What we did

We started by building rooms of our own: synthetic impulse responses with a single echo, several echoes, and millisecond-scale reflections with a decaying tail. Convolving a clap recording with them confirmed that convolution produces exactly the echoes we designed, and that an echo in time is a comb-shaped ripple in frequency.

Then we took the same clap recording into the two rooms above. A phone played it from the middle of each room while a hand-held recorder captured it at four positions, ①–④: the middle of each wall in the bedroom and the four corners in the bathroom (see the room maps). Because we know exactly what was played, we can deconvolve each recording to estimate the room's impulse response, then measure how fast it decays and how much energy arrives early.

What we found so far

The small, tiled bathroom holds on to sound longer at first and is less clear than the bedroom, at every one of the eight positions.

The bathroom's early decay time is 0.40 s against the bedroom's 0.32 s. Its clarity, the ratio of energy in the first 50 ms to everything after, is 7.6 dB against 9.7 dB. The ranges of the two rooms do not overlap. Split by frequency, the bedroom is clearest around 1 kHz, while above 2 kHz the two rooms are nearly the same.

We are careful with the word "because". The rooms differ in size, materials, openings, and where we put the phone and recorder, so any one of these could be responsible. We also found that the phones shape what we measure: the speaker's own frequency response appears in every recording, and the phone seems to apply automatic gain control, which breaks the linearity our model assumes. Fixing that, with a sine-sweep measurement and a controlled towels-on/towels-off test, is the plan for the next three weeks.
